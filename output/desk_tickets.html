Expected

Accounting. A discount request is a margin question before it is anything else. There is no point establishing whether we can source 20 hoodies if the price the club wants loses the shop money on every one. Accounting sets the floor, and everything else is negotiated inside it.

The delegations we expect

Reflection

What the three tickets taught us about evaluating the team, the design, and where the mesh does and does not pay for itself.

1 — How we evaluate agent performance, ticket by ticket

"Did the ticket close" is not a score, because a shop that touches real money can close a ticket the wrong way and still show green. We evaluate each ticket against four criteria, all of which were fixed before the agents ran, in the Expected sections of this document:

  • Did the team call the right specialist first, and exclude the obviously irrelevant ones? We pre-registered "who should not be involved" for every ticket precisely so a lazy "ask everyone" pattern would show up as a contradicted prediction, not a vague bad feeling.
  • Did it respect the hard policy numbers rather than guessing round ones? The 40% margin floor ($36.67 on the hoodie) and the lease's exact monthly_rent ($2,400.00) are both numbers a hallucinating agent would get approximately right and a correct one gets exactly right.
  • Did it report state honestly? pending is not paid, and a queued purchase order is not a shipment. This is the one that matters most, because the failure mode here is not "the agent was wrong" but "the agent lied to a customer or a supervisor about money that has not moved yet."
  • How many passes did it cost, and was that cost the problem's or ours? Ticket 102 closed in one pass at 11 requests, 10 tool calls, 27,903 tokens — a fixed two-hop chain with nothing to negotiate. Ticket 103 took 8 passes and was blocked three times, but the blocking was not the agents reasoning badly: they were correctly refusing to approve themselves a price override that the system had no tool for yet. Counting that against the agents' performance would have been the wrong conclusion — it was our harness that was incomplete, and we only know that because we checked the audit trail instead of just the final ticket status.

Ticket 101 sits in between: it needed exactly the agents we predicted, in exactly the order we predicted, but it also needed something no amount of good reasoning could shortcut — a human to click approve on invoice 501 before the vendor would ship at all. That is a correct two-run result, not an inefficient one, and the distinction only shows up if the evaluation asks "was the extra pass the agent's fault or the world's?" rather than just counting passes.

3 — What a single agent with tools would have handled just as well

Ticket 102 is the clean case. Read the lease, check the cash picture, file the payment request — a fixed, linear, two-tool-call sequence with no competing constraint to weigh and nobody outside the shop to write to. harness.md already calls it "the shortest chain of the three," and the audit trail backs that up: one pass, 11 requests, 10 tool calls. None of that number reflects genuine specialist judgment — Facilities and Accounting never disagreed about anything, they just each held one fact the other needed. A single agent carrying list_leases, get_payment_plan and request_payment directly would have produced the identical approvals row with less delegation bookkeeping and no risk of the depth cap or cycle guard ever mattering, because there was never a second opinion to seek.

The same logic applies to the purely mechanical half of ticket 101: the check_inventory call that discovers CC-TEE-WHITE / S is qty = 0 is a lookup, not a decision, and does not need an "Inventory agent" persona to execute correctly — any agent holding that one tool would find the same zero. What does need a dedicated Inventory specialist is the next step, deciding which vendor to retry against and whether the invoice block changes the plan, because that is where genuine cross-domain judgment (stock vs. vendor status vs. cash) actually lives.

The pattern: delegation earns its cost exactly where two domains' facts actually conflict or trade off against each other (103's margin-vs-stock-vs-cash three-way squeeze is the extreme case, at 8 passes). Where a ticket is really "look up one fact, check it against one threshold, file one request" — ticket 102, and the check_inventory half of 101 — a single tool-using agent gets there in fewer hops for the identical result.
4 — Three new problems our current team could already solve
  • An in-stock customer order we never exercised. A customer_order for, say, 2 × CC-HOOD-NAVY size L — a size that actually has stock, unlike the tee in ticket 101. check_inventory would find enough on hand, reserve_stock would commit it, and Customer Service would draft a confirmation. No vendor, no invoice, no approval at all — the "easy path" through the exact same tools, which this run of three tickets happened never to land on.
  • A second, unrelated overdue-invoice notice. Say Elm City Gifts (vendor 2, "mugs and small goods") sends a reminder on a bill the shop had forgotten. This is ticket 102's shape generalized from a lease to an invoice: Accounting runs list_invoices and get_vendor_status to confirm it is real and actually overdue, then request_payment queues it for a human click — the identical pattern proven on rent, now proven on a different vendor's invoice without touching a line of code.
  • A bulk-discount request that clears the margin floor outright. Ticket 103 only ever showed us the rejection side of quote_discount — a discount that fails the 40% floor and has to be talked down. A request for, say, 5 hoodies at 15% off would pass the ceiling on the first call, need no request_price_override, and resolve through Accounting and Inventory confirming stock, with Customer Service drafting a straightforward "yes." We have the tool for the approval path; we had just never watched it say yes.
5 — Three new problems our current team could not solve, and what we'd need to build
  • Anything involving money coming back in — a refund or a deposit. A customer wants to return a defective hoodie, or pay a deposit on a custom order. Our cash_accounts model is deliberately one-directional — harness.md states it plainly: "Revenue is not modeled, so cash only ever goes down." There is no returns table, no record_refund or record_cash_inflow tool, and request_payment's kind values only cover outflows (invoice, rent, purchase order). We would need a new returns table, a process_return tool, and a refund branch in the approval system that still gates on a human click — just in the other direction — plus a reason to trust Accounting (or a new Returns/Refunds role) with it.
  • Anything that genuinely has to be sent, not just drafted. A vendor that needs same-day written confirmation or cancels the order, or a customer who needs a real confirmation email today. draft_message only ever writes to the drafts table with status = 'draft', and by design — "no send path exists" is one of the ten safety guardrails in harness.md. A ticket whose resolution depends on the message actually leaving the building cannot finish; a human has to physically carry it out-of-band. We would need a real send_email (or SMS) tool wired to an actual provider — still sitting behind the same human-approval queue payments use, so an agent still cannot unilaterally contact a real person — and probably a broadened Customer Service or new Vendor Relations role authorized to use it.
  • Anything needing an alternate supplier when the usual vendor is unavailable. The vendors table has exactly three rows, each with a distinct specialty — Bulldog Print Co is the only apparel vendor, Elm City Gifts the only mugs-and-small-goods vendor, QuickShip CT the only courier. If Bulldog went on hold for two weeks instead of clearing in five days, find_restock_options has nowhere else to look — it is built to report one vendor's lead time, not choose among several. We would need the schema to support more than one vendor per specialty, a compare_vendor_options tool that weighs lead time against unit cost across candidates, and Inventory (or a new Procurement role) authorized to place a purchase order against something other than the default vendor.
